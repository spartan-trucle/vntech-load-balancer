Global load balancing

Between regions and continents. A local balancer picks a backend for traffic that already reached it; a global one has to change where the client sends traffic in the first place.

2.1 · What one region cannot fix

A balancer cannot fail over itself, and it cannot outrun the speed of light

  • 1 Your ALB lives inside the region it protects. When that region loses its control plane, nothing in-region is left to route around it.
  • 2 Ho Chi Minh City to us-east-1 is ~230 ms round trip. Even a perfect least-request balancer in Virginia gives back none of it. The only fix is to terminate the connection closer to the user.
  • why go global Latency · disaster recovery · capacity beyond one region · data residency (GDPR, Vietnam's Decree 53 — some data legally cannot leave).

2.3 · DNS-based routing (GSLB)

The same hostname answers differently per user — then gets out of the way completely

  • inputs Only four: who is asking (resolver IP + EDNS Client Subnet), health state from its own probes, a precomputed latency table, and your policy.
  • order Health filters first, then the policy runs on what is left: geo, latency, weighted, or primary/secondary.
  • omission It never sees load. A region that is healthy but saturated keeps its full share, because a health check answers "is it up", not "can it take more".

2.4 · Why DNS can never be fast

The TTL (time to live) is a promise about lookups, and an open socket never looks anything up

  • The GSLB's only moment of influence is the lookup. After it answers it cannot recall the answer, cannot notify anyone, cannot even find out who is holding one.
  • don't "Just set TTL to 1 second." Resolvers enforce floors anyway, you pay per query, and cached answers are what keep users working when your nameservers are unreachable.
  • do Make the slowness harmless. Never cut the old address off: let it proxy onward, or answer 503 + Retry-After. Users can see zero errors during a failover that still takes five minutes.

2.5 · Anycast

Announce one address from many places and let the internet do the routing

  • how Several sites each announce the same prefix over BGP — "traffic for this address can come to me" — and every network on the path picks whichever announcement looks closest to it.
  • failover Withdraw the announcement and the internet re-learns the next-best path in seconds. No client needs a new answer: the address never changed.
  • gotcha BGP counts networks crossed, not milliseconds, and you cannot say "send this PoP 20% of traffic". You also need your own ASN (autonomous system number), address block and peering — which is why most teams rent anycast.

2.6 · Anycast + edge proxy

Put a real L7 proxy in the nearby PoP and the handshakes stop crossing the ocean

  • the point The PoP is not a signpost. It completes the connection itself, then forwards the request over a warm pooled connection on a private backbone. Cloudflare, Google's global LB and Fastly all work this way.
  • control is back Because it is a real L7 proxy, everything from section 1 now works globally: health-aware origin choice, retry into another region, weighted regional canaries, graceful drain of a whole region.
  • cdn A CDN is this same machine with a cache bolted on. Note the handshake win lands even on requests that can never be cached — which is why an API behind a CDN pays off at a 0% hit rate.

2.7 · HTTP redirection

Your own code can say "go to Singapore", and the client pays for a second connection

  • how Answer the first request with 302 and Location: https://sg.shop.vn/. The client drops this connection and starts over on the regional hostname.
  • upside The redirector is your code, so it decides on what DNS never sees: the logged-in account, its home region under GDPR or Decree 53, live load. Linux download mirrors pick a server this way.
  • gotcha The client asks once. Redirect once per session, never with 301 (browsers cache it with no expiry), and give every regional hostname its own failover.

2.10 · Capacity

Failover moves the traffic, not the capacity. Size each region for the whole world.

peak 200,000 req/s global
one region down → survivor takes 200,000
one L7 balancer node: 25,000 req/s at 60% CPU (measure yours)
200,000 ÷ 25,000 = 8 nodes
+ lose 1 AZ of 3 → × 1.5 = 12
per region: 12 nodes, normally at ~33% of rated load
  • the real trap If Tokyo cannot take 100% right now, you have turned one region outage into two.
  • Run the balancer tier at 30–40% in normal times. That idle headroom is what makes failover a non-event.
  • Count connections too, not only requests: 400,000 open WebSockets × ~50 KB is 20 GB of memory across the fleet.
  • Then prove it: game-day drill, drain one region on purpose in business hours, watch p99 and 5xx.
