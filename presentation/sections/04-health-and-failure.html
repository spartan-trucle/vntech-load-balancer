Health & failure

A balancer is only as good as its idea of which backends are alive. How it finds out, how fast, and what it does during deploys.

4.1 · Active health checks

Check every 5 s, mark down after 3 failures: users eat 13 seconds of errors

GET /healthz every 5 s · timeout 2 s · unhealthy after 3 failures · healthy after 2 passes
  • Worst-case detection time ≈ interval × unhealthy threshold + timeout. Shorter interval = faster, but 200 pods × 10 balancer nodes × 1 check/s is 2,000 health requests a second.
  • Real traffic noticed first: the pod failed 5 user requests in a row in the first 200 ms. Next slide.

4.2 · Passive checks · outlier detection

Watch the real responses. Eject a pod after 5 errors in a row, and let it back in later.

# Envoy outlier_detection (these are the defaults)
consecutive_5xx:        5      # 5 errors in a row → eject
interval:               10s    # how often it re-evaluates
base_ejection_time:     30s    # × number of times ejected
max_ejection_percent:   10     # never eject more than 10%

# log
12:00:07.020  pod-c  503  1
12:00:07.061  pod-c  503  2
          ...
12:00:07.180  pod-c  503  5 → ejected for 30 s
12:00:37.180  pod-c  back in pool
12:00:37.400  pod-c  503  → ejected for 60 s
  • active Probes a URL on a timer. Finds pods that are down before users do, but slowly.
  • passive Counts errors on real traffic. Reacts in milliseconds, but only after some users already got a 5xx.
  • Use both. Active tells you a new pod is ready; passive catches the one that looks healthy on /healthz but fails real requests.
  • Why the 10% cap? If a bad deploy breaks every pod, ejecting them all leaves nothing. Capping keeps some capacity serving.

4.3 · What should /healthz check?

If the health check calls the database, one slow database takes every pod out of the pool

readiness checks the DB · the DB has a 10 s slowdown
12:00:00  db  p99 → 8 s
12:00:05  pod-a  /healthz  timeout  1
12:00:05  pod-b  /healthz  timeout  1
12:00:05  pod-c  /healthz  timeout  1
          ...
12:00:15  0 of 3 pods healthy
12:00:15  every request → 503  full outage
# the DB recovered at 12:00:10
two endpoints, two questions
AnswersChecks
/livez"Should I be restarted?"Process is up, event loop not stuck. Nothing external.
/readyz"Can I take traffic?"Warm-up done, not draining. Local state only.
  • A dependency that is down for everyone is not a reason to remove one pod. Handle it with timeouts and fallbacks in the request path.
  • Balancers know this trap: ALB routes to all targets if all fail, Envoy stops trusting health below 50% healthy (panic mode).

4.4 · Draining during a deploy

Kubernetes stops the pod and removes it from the balancer at the same time. The balancer finds out last.

  • Draining = stop sending new requests, let in-flight ones finish, then remove. ALB calls it deregistration delay (default 300 s — often lower it to 30 s).
  • In Kubernetes add preStop: sleep 10 and keep terminationGracePeriodSeconds above it, so the pod outlives the balancer's view of it.
  • The reverse happens on start: send traffic only after /readyz passes, and use slow start (ALB: 30–900 s) so a cold JVM isn't handed a full share on second one.

4.5 · Retries at the balancer

Retrying on another pod hides one bad pod. Retrying at every layer turns one slow DB into 27× the load.

  • do Retry once, on a different pod, only for idempotent requests (GET, or a POST with an idempotency key) and only for connect errors or 503.
  • do Retry at one layer, usually the one closest to the failing service.
  • budget Cap retries as a share of traffic: Envoy's retry budget allows retries up to 20% of active requests. When everything fails, retries stop instead of multiplying.
  • don't Retry a POST /payments that timed out. The first attempt may have charged the card.
